In classic strategy card games (using generic, IP-safe mechanics like *Resource Pool*, *Exhaust/Ready*, *Continuous*, and *Instant*), bad players lose because they lock all their resources into dead weight and take massive "hull drag" damage every turn. Master players win by building an **Engine**—combining small, early plays that compound into massive mid-game momentum.

## Part 0: Core Rules, The 3-Turn Demo, & Modular Onboarding (`P:DD` System)

### The Core Game Rule: Why There Is No Magic Money Tree (It's a River)

In this game, Conventional Thinkers play a **"Zero-Sum Land Deck"**—they believe wealth is a fixed pie of residential dirt and tax revenue. They hoard *Dormant Land*, let *22%* of their Cohort take early damage, and hemorrhage **NZD 17.7B to 28.5B** in *Fiscal Hull Drag* every turn until their Crown Debt hits **200%** and they lose the game.

The **IGWelfare Deck** teaches players that human capability is not a zero-sum pie; it is a **compounding creative fountain**. You don't need a "magic money tree" when you stop burning 75% of your treasury on preventable crises and unlock the gold already standing in the river.

### How This Plays in a Classroom or Workshop (The "3-Turn Demo")

As a 20-minute interactive game with students, young voters, or policy officials, the demo runs in three rounds:

1. **Round 1 (Displacement Mode / `SS0`):** Players try to survive using only conventional tools (raising PAYE taxes, cutting infrastructure maintenance, or relying on high-churn replacement migration). They watch the *Oranga Tamariki / Hospital / Justice drag* eat their budget while debt spirals toward 200% of GDP.
2. **Round 2 (Single-Lens Pitch-Pole):** Players try playing *only* early-childhood spending without taxing land wealth or changing Treasury's cash-flow accounting (`T:01`, the *Scotland scenario*), or *only* industrial growth without land-value recycling (`T:02`, the *1989 Tokyo scenario*). They see firsthand why the boat nose-dives when one foil is missing.
3. **Round 3 (Up on Foils / `SS0 -> SS1 -> SS2`):** Players link the coupled hydrofoil levers (`B:01`–`B:09`) with the Nobel Laureate cards (`N:01`–`N:06`), *Kaupapa* Personas (`K:01`–`K:07`), and `QEA-2026` Electrification (`E:01`–`E:03`)—unlocking the Year 5 actuarial breakeven, surviving the Orthodoxy Hazards (`H:01`–`H:05`), and landing the `SS2` PAYE tax cut and infrastructure harvest.

  * **Summary:** *Every single median wealth group across all 20 faces of the die is better off* (`+20.49%` national welfare gain, active entrepreneurs ahead up to NZD 100M, NZ Super at 65, and PAYE cut to 18.5%).
  * **Result:** *Roll any number. You win.*
* **Flavor Text:** *"Beware the Blindfold of Orthodoxy, used to play economic Russian roulette where more than 1 in 6 lose in the name of 'market efficiency.' True systems design rewrites the physics of the board so every roll of a child's life comes up a winner."*

## Epilogue: Why I Know This Game Works in the Real World**

Repetition is a form of emphasis.  You can read this story in [`CREDITS.md`](../CREDITS.md), but to make sure it stays with the rules, we repeat it here.

In 1987, getting a compiler to generate code for a new microprocessor architecture cost millions of dollars and took years to accomplish. I found that I could port the GNU C compiler to a new architecture in a matter of weeks (and later days, and sometimes hours), generating code that outperformed the vendor's own compiler. I was convinced that such a competitive advantage warranted commercial success, but the idea was absolutely rejected by the economic and entrepreneurial orthodoxy of the day, for two reasons:

1. *"Nobody will pay money for free software."*  
2. *"Everybody knows it's illegal to use the Internet for commerce—only research."*

The fact that nobody thought it would succeed (after I pitched the idea for two years) convinced me that we would have no competition—and with no competition, how could we lose? With that confidence, I recruited two co-founders who went "all-in" with me, each matching the USD 2,000 that was all the money in my checking account.

We bent the curve in two ways: (1) I argued that as a Stanford graduate student, it was legitimate research to test whether we could bootstrap a company selling support for free software using the Internet for support and distribution (`B:04`), and (2) we positioned the company—Cygnus Support—with the tagline: *"We make free software affordable"* (`N:01`).

When we started Cygnus in 1989, I used to joke—in all seriousness—that someday an economist would win the Nobel Prize for explaining the theoretical basis of why our company worked. As I later detailed in *The (awesome) economics of open source*, open source exceeded expectations yet again when not one, but four Nobel Prizes in Economics validated the exact mechanisms we were pioneering in practice:

* By removing artificial barriers and optimizing transaction costs and institutional governance, we were realizing the theories of **Ronald Coase (Nobel Prize, 1991 — Card `N:01`)** and **Oliver Williamson (Nobel Prize, 2009 — Card `N:03`)**.  
* Even more importantly, open source proved the power of polycentric, community-led governance of the commons—the foundational insight for which **Elinor Ostrom (Nobel Prize, 2009 — Card `N:04`)** shared the 2009 prize with Williamson. Together, those governance principles provided an architecture for enabling the collective intelligence of the world to work together: an extraordinary force-multiplier that allowed our scrappy company and the global community to build vast libraries of software that have powered multiple scientific and commercial revolutions, artificial intelligence among them.
